01 · Wood 木 · 生

Spark

I love arranging my home and hosting people in it. But my sofa at home is long and straight, and I noticed that whenever more people come over, conversations naturally split along the seating. Silence is fine. Being overlooked is not. I’ve been the overlooked one before, and I didn’t want anyone to feel that way in my space.

So I wanted to design a sofa that is modular, striking, with a Rick Owens edge, and quietly pulls people into conversation.

Entertainment has become participatory, but the furniture that hosts it is still passive.

02 · Fire 火 · 长

Expand

To understand who we were designing for, we built two personas. Alex, 27, hosts often but finds modular sofas boxy and stylish ones uncomfortable. Maya, 32, is tired of a sofa that only works in one layout and has to be replaced after every move.

We then mapped existing sofas on two axes: how reconfigurable and how social they are, and how visually distinctive they are. We set out to design for the corner where adaptability, sociability, and a striking form meet.

03 · Earth 土 · 化

Turn

My part: Ideation

This was the turning point: translating what we learned into what Polarity had to be. Through mind-mapping and How Might We questions, we distilled four design criteria.

Social connectivity

A circular arrangement, so everyone faces in.

Striking

An aesthetically unique, sculptural form.

Comfort

Made for long-duration lounging.

Modularity

Pieces that can be added or removed.

I explored many directions in sketches and quick 3D forms, from single sculptural seats to full rings of seating, before we committed to one module that could curve into a circle.

04 · Metal 金 · 收

Refine

My part: Sketches, 3D modeling, fabric, and pleating

One module, many gatherings

I refined our direction into a single module: a curved aluminum shell holding a soft, pleated cushion. Repeated, rotated, or flipped, the same module forms an arc, a row, or a full circle.

3D modeling

I modeled the full system in Blender, arranging eight modules into a conversational arc, and hand-sculpted the pleated upholstery on each module.

Fabric and pleating

Finding the fabric. I collected samples from fabric stores. Some came pre-ribbed, while others were flat and needed to be pleated by hand.

The target. Our rendering shows the soft, organic pleats we were aiming for, a texture that is almost always made by hand.

First attempt. Our first folding experiment accidentally turned into something that looked like a face mask. Folding by hand gave the right look, but the pleats wouldn’t stay in place.

The method. I turned to fabric gathering, a sewing technique that pulls fabric along a stitched line. After many rounds of testing on the sewing machine, the pleats finally held their shape on the prototype.

Engineering and cost

We documented the system in a bill of materials and dimensioned drawings. Each module came to an estimated $266 in materials.

Prototype

The prototype brought the aluminum shell and the gathered upholstery together. A set of eight scale modules let us test how the system rearranges.

05 · Water 水 · 藏

Hold

Building Polarity taught me as much through its problems as its solutions.

Scale

We planned to CNC-mill a full-size mold for the shell, but even our school’s largest machine wasn’t big enough. Next time, I would design for fabrication from the start, splitting the shell into joinable segments.

Material

Aluminum gave us the sculptural shell we wanted, but it made each module heavy, which works against the whole idea of a sofa you can rearrange and flip. Lighter materials like molded plywood or fiberglass are worth exploring.

Market

We designed for people in their twenties and thirties, but the material and hand-finished pleats would make Polarity a high-end product. Having researched the pleating process myself, I saw firsthand how much handwork each module would require. Moving from hand-folding to machine gathering was a first step toward making the pleats reproducible, and pushing that further could bring the cost down. The idea might also fit hotel lobbies, galleries, and shared living spaces, places built for gathering strangers.

These are still open questions, and they are where the next version of Polarity begins.
